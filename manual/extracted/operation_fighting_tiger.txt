new weapons, naval task forces and some new missions to challenge you. This 
manual contains complete descriptions of each theater — the history, the 
forces involved, the events leading up to the war and the objectives you must 
accomplish to help your side win. In addition, there are sections on mission 
planning, electronic warfare as it relates to the F-16, the FSX (a modified 
version of the F-16 which your squadron will fly in the Kurile campaign) and 
a reference section detailing the new aircraft, weapons, ships and SAM 
systems in the game. 
Since Operation: Fighting Tiger is an add-on module for Falcon 3.0, almost all 
of the cockpit controls, the Campaign screens and other major portions of the 
game work exactly the same. A few enhancements have been made which will 
affect the whole game (which are described in the Falcon 3.01 section), but 
most relate directly to one or more of the new theaters.

FALCON 3.01 VS. FALCON 3.0 
When vou first run Falcon 3.01, you should notice a few changes right away, 
like our new company logo and a new title screen. If you have any doubts as 
to whether you are playing Falcon 3.01 or Falcon 3.0, look for the version 
aumber on the lower nght-hand corner of the title screen. It should read 
V3.01. The following list summarizes those differences between Falcon 3.0 
and Falcon 3.01 which affect the entire program, not just the new Operation: 
Fighting Tiger theaters. Please refer to Section II: Theaters of Conflict to 
learn about the Kurile Islands, Korea or Kashmir campaigns. 
New digitized sounds for old and new radio messages — There are several 
new radio messages and new digitized speech samples to go with them. In 
addition, many of the original radio messages which previously used the “radio 
static” sound cue have been given digitized speech samples. Note that a few 
radio messages still use the generic “radio static” sound cue. A list of the new 
messages and what they mean is given in Section [V: Mission Planning. 
New wingman commands — Three new wingman commands have been 
added to help you better control your flight. These commands allow you to 
spread out your formation, to tighten up your formation or to send your flight 
home without you. These commands are described both later in this chapter 
and in Section IV: Mission Planning. 
New planes in Red Flag — All the new planes added for Operation: Fighting 
Tiger will also be found in Red Flag. Note that you can only fly the F-16 in 
Red Flag. Ifthe FSX is added to a Red Flag mission, it will be flown by a non- 
squadron pilot. Turn to Section VIII: Reference for information on all the 
new aircraft in the game. 
Color changes — The sky and terrain colors for the new theaters have been 
adjusted. In addition, the takeoff time for the last mission of the day has been 
pushed back to 11 P.M. in all theaters. You will find that the world is much 
darker at this hour and you will have to rely more heavily on your cockpit instruments to fly and fight. 
New TWI codes — The TWI has new codes to designate the many new surface radar systems in the game. Changes in the codes will affect the original theaters, but the air defense weapons present in these theaters have not changed. Refer to Section VI: Electronic Warfare for a definition of the new TWI codes. In addition, the TWI will now detect radar systems that are in search mode, not just radars which have a lock on your aircraft.

Landing aids — A new, more realistic mode has been added to the ILS. In addition, VAS] lights have been added to the airfields in the new theaters. See the section on “Instrument and visual landing aids” later = in this chapter for 
more information. 
More weather — The weather can now be overcast. Check the “New 3 . Z : features” section later in this chapter for details. 
Improved debriefing — The Debriefing Screen provides more information, 
including whether you or any of your squadron pilots crashed into the ground 
and, if you fail a mission, an explanation why. See Section V: Tactical 
Operations for an explanation of the new debriefing information. 
Communications — To play Falcon 3.01 over a modem, network or direct 
connection, all players must be using the same version of the game. There are 
too many differences between Falcon 3.01 and Falcon 3.0d (or earlier) for 
reliable communications to be established, even in the old theaters. 
EMS used for digitized sounds — Falcon 3.01 uses EMS slightly differently 
than Falcon 3.0. Although there are many more digitized radio messages, this 
feature does not require any more EMS than it did before (1MB). You may 
notice, however, that the longest ACMI tape that you can record is now a bit 
shorter. Also, if you have only slightly more than the minimum required 
expanded memory for digitized speech, you might find that some EMS 
options on the System Setup Screen which were available to you in Falcon 3.0 
can no longer be turned on when you run Fa/con 3.01. 
AVTRv1.1— ACMI in Fakon 3.01 uses version 1.1 ofthe AVTR. You will 
not be able to replay tapes recorded with a different version of the AVTR.

History 
Since World War II, relations between Russia and Japan have been troubled 
by Cold War politics and by the Soviet occupation of a fewrocky islands which 
Japan claims as its own rightful property. The disputed islands are part of a 
chain stretching from the northern Japanese island of Hokkaido to the 
Kamchatka peninsula of the Russian Republic. Over the past two centuries, 
the islands in this chain have been bought, traded and stolen several times by 
both countries. The current dispute concers only the four islands closest to 
Japan: Etorofu, Kunashiri, Shikotan and Habomai. 
Relations between Russia and Japan first began in the early 19th century when 
Russia sought to expand its foreign trade. At first willfully isolationist, the 
feudal society of Japan quickly learned imperial ambitions and joined the 
international competition for power going on in the region between Russia, 
China and a handful of European nations. Just as quickly, Japan became very 
good at this competition, dispatching its rivals in a series of wars to gain control 
of large tracts of Russia and China as well as all of Korea. Japan continued 
pursuing its imperial ambitions until its defeat in World War I.

Geography 
The Kurile Islands form a rocky chain running from Hokkaido to the Kamchatka peninsula and mark the border between the Pacific Ocean and the 
Sea of Okhotsk. The four southernmost islands of the chain, Etorofu, 
Kunashiri, Shikotan and Habomai, are the objects of the territorial dispute. 
The islands themselves are not much more than barren chunks of rock, but 
they fence one of two vital straits giving the Russian fleet access to Pacific 
waters. The T'sushima strait to the south, which nuns between South Korea 
and Japan, could be easily blocked in the event of hostilities. If Russia were to 
relinquish the four disputed islands, free movement of its fleet through the 
northern strait would also be jeopardized, particularly in the winter when the 
ice pack moves south. 
Logistics 
Neither combatant’s economy is at much risk from the war. Hokkaido has 
some industry, but Japan’s major production centers are safely to the south. 
Military supplies for both sides are produced and stockpiled outside the 
theater. Supplies for troops on the Kurile Islands are subject to interdiction, 
however, and Russia in particular might find it difficult to maintain supply 
lines. 
Order of battle 
Russia has deployed a strong garrison force occupying well-prepared fortifi- 
cations on each of the disputed islands. In addition, Etorofu and Kunashiri 
have airfields capable of supporting all types of Russian military aircraft. 
Currently only a squadron of MiG-23s is stationed at Etorofu, but additional 
aircraft are being deployed as part of the Russian reinforcement. The only 
other major enemy airbase in the theater is on Sakhalin Island, but Russian 
planes, including the new MiG-31, can easily reach the area from bases 
outside the theater. In addition, the Admiral Kuznetsov aircraft carer, which 
carries two squadrons of MiG-29s and another of Su-27s, is operating in the 
northern Pacific and could enter the theater at any time. Intelligence data 
Suggests that the Russians might also unleash an experimental fighter aircraft 
designated the UMF. Little is known about this weapon, but it is apparently 
a robot aircraft which blurs the line between guided missile and fighter. All 
the known information about this vehicle is given in Section VIII: Reference.

To break the deadlock, General Douglas MacArthur made a risky but brilliantly successful amphibious assault on Inchon, 200 miles behind North Korean lines. A hastily thrown together U.S. X Corps cut off the DPRK invasion force from its supplies, while the U.S. Eighth Army mounted a counteroffensive from Busan. The offensive shattered the DPRK army in two weeks. U.S. forces pushed past the 38th parallel and advanced deep into North Korea with the aim of unifying the country. 
Once again U.S. strategic ambivalence about Korea surfaced. US. strategists 
considered the Korean conflict to be “the wrong war, in the wrong place, with the wrong enemy.” The last thing they wanted was to waste resources needed to fight the Soviet Union in Europe by fighting the Chinese in Korea. General MacArthur, who disagreed with the State Department's focus on Europe, practically dared the Chinese communists to enter the war by marching his troops toward the Chinese border with open flanks and scant reconnaissance. 
The Chinese took advantage of MacArthur’s blunder and, using sheer 
numbers to make up for inferior weapons and training, drove the U.N. troops back below the 38th parallel. The Chinese army captured Seoul, with only a 
lack of supplies preventing it from advancing farther. Fresh reinforcements 
allowed the U.N. forces to retake Seoul and establish a defensive front at the 
38th parallel. The situation was again at a stalemate, and the U.N. faceda long, 
grinding war of attrition against a huge Chinese army, 
In the spring of 1951, the Chinese resolved to inflict such heavy casualties that 
the U.N. would abandon Korea. Their tactic, however, was to assault well- 
equipped, well-prepared U.N. positions en masse, which resulted only in huge 
numbers of casualties on the Chinese side. A brief U.N. counteroffensive 
established the current border between North and South Korea. The cease- 
fire was signed on July 27, 1953.

coast to the other. ‘The two sides have maintained an uneasy truce, but shots

discovers tunnels being dug underneath the DMZ by the North. The U.S. has 
maintained a strong military presence in the South as deterrence to any further 
incursions by the North.

Logistics 
Both countries have well developed, virtually self-sufficient defense industries

choice, waypoint setup, fuel consumption, formations, putting together 
multiple flight strike forces and target attack techniques.

capabilities, then use your strengths against your opponent’s weaknesses. The key to a successful offense is to concentrate overwhelming force on the point you choose to attack. The key to a successful defense is aggressiveness.

compromise between avoiding the worst enemy air defenses and conforming 
to the capabilities of available weaponry. 
Standoff attacks 
As its name implies, a standoff attack is launched outside the range of enemy 
air defense weapons. By definition, a standoff attack is the safest of any attack 
method, but only a few weapons have full standoff capability. There are 
roughly three range umbrellas of anti-aircraft defenses to deal with. The 
smallest umbrella is that of AAA and SA-7 SAMs. Even general purpose iron 
bombs can be dropped from outside this umbrella, and a standoff method of 
attack can be used with any weapon except LAUs, Durandals, high-drag 
bombs and guns. The second umbrella, that of medium-range SAMs like the 
SA-6, the SA-N-9 and the SA-N-4, extends about 15-20 nm. Only the 
Harpoon anti-ship missile and the HARM can be launched from outside this 
range. The largest umbrella, that ofthe SA-N-6 and SA-4 SAMs, approaches 
the range of the Harpoon at about 50 nm. A standoff attack can still be 
conducted inside these larger umbrellas, but you must use time rather than 
distance to avoid enemy SAMs. The key is to fly underneath radar to avoid 
detection until reaching maximum weapon range. Then, pop up just high 
enough to acquire the target, fire your weaponsas fast as possible and dive back 
into cover before the enemy SAM operators have time to lock onto your 
aircraft and fire their own weapons.

SET UP FLIGHTS 
Number and size 
There is a trade-off between the number of flights you can create and the size 
of those flights. In combat, it is often better to use a number of srnall flights 
rather than a single large one. The advantages are that in assigning each flight 
a particular portion of the mission, you can set up its waypoints and weaponry 
so that each flight complements the others. The disadvantage is that you can 
only lead one flight. The pilots in the other flights will follow the flight plan 
you create to the letter, so effective planning becomes even more crucial to 
success. 
For some air-to-air missions, such as a fighter sweep, you might want to lead 
a four-ship formation, but thatis probably the largest flight you should assign. 
On air-to-ground missions, it is definitely better to use two-ship formations. 
With extra flights available, you can assign a flight or two to take out anti- 
aircraft batteries and another to deal with enemy fighters. Having several 
flights attacking a target from different directions will make the job of enemy 
SAM operators more difficult since they will not be able to track a single 
formation and pick off the aircraft one by one.

Standoff attacks

so that you keep as much distance between yourself and the air defenses 
around your target as possible. The longer the range of the weapon, the better 
this works.

waypoints allow a flight to approach its target underneath the enemy air search 
radar, pop up at the maximum weapon range to acquire the target, fire its 
weapons and break off back to low altitude, all before the enemy has time to react. While human pilots might be able to perform this mission with only a little help from preset waypoints, computer-controlled wingmen need explicit directions for all phases of the mission. LEADING THE ATTACK

Violations and consequences 
Any hostile act against a unit proscribed by the rules of engagement will 
constitute a violation. The launching of weapons targeted at an enemy unut, 
even if the weapons do not hit their mark, will be considered a hostile act. For 
example, if the engagement rule for air forces allows attacks only in self- 
defense, firing on an enemy aircraft will be a violation if you shoot first. Only 
after the enemy aircraft shoots at you or another allied unit will it become fair 
game. This does not give you license to attack any enemy unit, however. If an 
enemy aircraft shoots at you, for example, you are still not allowed to attack 
enemy ground or naval forces. 
The immediate consequence of committing a hostile act is that enemy forces 
will attack more readily. They might even come looking for the pilot whe 
initiated the hostilities. If this is the case, the AWACS crew should pick up 
an increase in enemy air activity. Expect to see some interceptors soon. On the 
other hand, if you get a message from the AWACS authorizing you to engage 
and destroy enemy aircraft, you can assume things have gotten out of hand and 
that both sides will freely engage and destroy each other's forces 
When (orif) you get back from your mission, the pilot responsible for breaking 
the rules of engagement will face a court -martial. If the infracnon was minor, 
he may be let off, but otherwise he will be drummed out of the service and you 
will lose a good pilot. 
Another possible consequence of violating the n les of engagement ls that the 
level of hostilities between U.S. and Russtan forces could escalate. Some 
violations are more serious than others. If you shoot down a Russian fighter 
who was out looking for trouble over allied cerntory, hosulines are less likely

INTRODUCTION 
Flying an F-16, you are mostly on the receiving end of electronic Warfare 
(EW), but there are a few things you can do to fight back. As far as the F-1¢ 
‘s concerned, the important part of EW involves detection and targeting 
signals coming fom aircraft and surface radars. Fa/con 3.01 includes many 
new radar threats. The following section describes the measures you can take 
to avoid becoming the victim of one of these new threats. 
THE ALR-69 THREAT WARNING SYSTEM 
The F-16 and FSX are equipped with the ALR-69 Threat Warning System, 
avery sophisticated passive radar receiver which sifts through the electromag- 
netic spectrum to identify signals representing potential threats. The system 
can detect signals through any of seven antennas, providing full 360° threat 
warning. The ALR-69 can determine the radar type, the signal strength and 
the bearing ofa threat. It can also determine ifa missile has been launched and 
whether a threat radar is transmitting missile guidance commands. The 
Threat Warning Indicator (TWI) is the primary display for the ALR-69. 
(The LCK and LNH lights are the other system displays.) The ALR-69 
information is provided to the pilot in the form of symbols displayed on the 
TWL. The shape of a symbol, its position on the TWI and its numeric code 
each provide a key part of the profile for a threat picked up by the ALR-69. 
Threat type 
The shape of a TWI symbol indicates the type of the threat. Diamond 
symbols designate aircraft radar, large squares designate surface radar and 
small squares designate missiles. Diamond and large square symbols also 
contain a numeric code which indicates the specific capabilities of the radar 
threat. The numeric codes indicating the types of radar used by aircraft threats 
are explained in the Fa/con 3.0 manual. The codes for surface radar systems are 
new, however, and are given in the following table.

ENEMY SAM OPERATORS 
While you might only see the flames and hurtling steel, behind the controls 
of every surface-to-air missile is a human being. SAM operators are not 
perfect. Like the rest of us they get overworked, they can focus too sharply on 
one target and they worry about dying too soon. The less experienced the 
SAM crew, the more likely they will suffer from target overload and target 
fixation. They will take longer to get their systems operational when anairraid 
warning is sounded and will take longer to fire their missiles once they have 
acquired a target. The more work you create for the SAM crews, the less 
effective they will be. Having multiple flights crossing the target from 
different directions can confuse the operators, making it difficult for them : 
get off even a single good shot. Inexperienced crews will tend to concentrate 
all their fire on the flight leader, which is you.

In reality, the FSX still faces challenges to its completion. It has already hit a 
couple of bumps which have made the project two years late and well over 
budget, and faces a few more down the road. The main obstacles the project 
had to overcome were objections by the U.S. Congress to the transfer of 
defense and aerospace technologies to Japan. The Department of Defense still 
has not approved the transfer of the source code for the flight and weapons 
control computers, although a deal has been worked out to mollity the 
Congress on other issues by giving the American company, General Dynam- 
ics, a greater share of the production contracts. Because of the overdrawn 
budget and the conditions added to the deal by the U.S. Congress, the FSX 
project faces political challenges in Japan as well. 
The FSX project began as an effort to replace Japan's aging F-1 fighter 
Mainly for financial reasons, the F-16 was chosen as a base model over the F- 
15, the F/A-18 and a proposal to build a completely new all-Japanese plane, 
The project began in 1987. The current plan calls for the first test flight of 
aircraft in early 1996 and the completion of the first production FSX in 1999

The Sky Watch is a large phased-array radar mounted on the Kuznetsoy 
aircraft carer. The system utilizes chirp technology and a set of four large 
fixed antenna to extend the range over the horizon. A chirp radar gets its name by continually varying the radar frequency, which extends the range and 
makes the demodulated radar signal sound like a bird’s chirp. Normally the 
range of a pulse radar is limited by the distance the radar signal can travel in 
the time between the pulses. Beyond this range, the radar operator cannot tell 
whether a radar return is reflecting off a distant target illuminated by the first 
pulse or reflecting off a closer target illuminated by the second pulse. A chirp 
radar can more easily distinguish between two such returns because the second 
pulse has a different frequency than the first pulse. Chirp radars are also less 
susceptible to ECM. 
The Sky Watch is used for many purposes aboard the Kuznetsov, including 
air search, intercept control, early warning and target handoff to SAM and 
AAA systems.
